    How this schedule was built

    • Stimulation usually runs 8–14 days; this plan uses about 11, starting day 2–3. Trigger to retrieval is exactly 34–36 hours — the one interval that does not move.
    • PGT-A results take 7–14 days, so this is a freeze-all cycle with the transfer in a later, separately prepped month rather than a fresh transfer.
    • Dating. After a day-5 blastocyst transfer you are already 2 weeks 5 days pregnant, so the due date is the transfer date plus 261 days. Every prenatal appointment below is placed off that clock, and the week chip on each row shows the gestational age it lands on.
    • Prenatal cadence is the standard one: two betas 48 hours apart, viability and heartbeat scans, graduation from the fertility clinic around 9 weeks, then OB visits every 4 weeks to 28 weeks, every 2 weeks to 36, and weekly until delivery.
    • Every clinic and every body runs a little different, and dates slip — that is normal. Edit them as yours land. This is a planning tool, not medical advice; your clinic's schedule always wins.

              History of lullabies

              Four thousand years of adults singing at babies who will not go to sleep.

              BeginningsThe oldest one we can still read

              The oldest lullaby anyone can still read was pressed into clay in Mesopotamia around four thousand years ago, written in Akkadian. It is not a gentle song. The baby's crying has disturbed the god of the house, and the singer tells the child, in so many words, to be quiet. There is a distinct edge of threat in it. Whoever composed it was extremely tired.

              That is the remarkable part. Across four millennia, the oldest surviving example of the form is immediately recognisable — an adult, awake in the middle of the night, singing something slightly desperate at a baby who will not settle.

              Written lullabies are far younger than sung ones. Music long predates writing: the oldest instruments anyone agrees on are bone flutes from caves in southern Germany, carved from vulture wing bone and mammoth ivory roughly forty thousand years ago. People were making tuned instruments tens of thousands of years before anyone kept a record of anything. Nobody can say when the first person hummed at an infant — only that it was a very long time before someone thought to write it down.

              By the classical world the habit was ordinary enough to be worth commenting on. Plato, in the Laws, noticed that nurses never simply put a baby down. They rocked it and sang at the same time, and he argued the motion and the sound were doing the same job.

              LanguageEvery language named it after the same sound

              The English word is two soothing noises stuck together. Lull comes from Middle English lullen, to quiet with sounds, and it is imitative — it is simply a written-down version of the noise itself. Lullaby appears in English from the sixteenth century.

              The same thing happened independently almost everywhere else. Italian ninna nanna. Spanish nana. Turkish ninni. Russian bayu. Korean jajang. None of these are translations of one another. Each is a separate language writing down the same soft, repetitive sound that adults make at babies, and then using that sound as the name for the entire genre.

              It is one of the few places you can watch a word being born directly out of a noise, in a dozen unrelated languages at once.

              HonestyThe dark ones, and why they exist

              If you go through traditional lullabies expecting sweetness throughout, they will surprise you.

              The Coventry Carol, from a sixteenth-century English mystery play, is a lullaby in every formal sense — soft, rocking, in a minor key. It is sung by the mothers of Bethlehem to their babies just before Herod's soldiers arrive. It is one of the most beautiful melodies in the English language and it is about a massacre.

              Elsewhere: a little grey wolf waits at the edge of the bed in Russia, Cuca the alligator-witch is on her way in Brazil, and Irish verses reassure the child that the fairies will not take them tonight. Some lullabies are frank about the singer's own exhaustion. Some are about poverty. A few are openly about wishing the baby would sleep so that the adult might also sleep.

              There is a good explanation for this, and it is rather moving. A lullaby is one of the very few socially permitted places to say a hard thing out loud — sung gently, in a quiet room, to the one person present who cannot understand a word of it. The tune stays soothing no matter what the words are carrying. So the words ended up carrying the things people could not say anywhere else.

              EverywhereWhat stayed the same

              Harvard's Natural History of Song project spent five years assembling recordings from more than sixty societies, and then measured them. Lullabies turned out to carry a signature you can pick out acoustically: slower, lower, breathier and rhythmically simpler than the same culture's other songs.

              They also ran it in the other direction. Given short clips from unfamiliar traditions, listeners who knew nothing whatsoever about the music could still pick out which songs were lullabies, well above chance. Later work played lullabies from entirely foreign traditions to infants and watched them relax — pupils narrowing, heart rate settling — to songs from cultures they had never once encountered.

              The words differ enormously. The scales differ. The instruments differ. The way a person sings a baby to sleep does not.

              NowLullabies people are writing today

              None of this is a museum piece. In 2011 Carnegie Hall began pairing pregnant women and new parents — starting with young mothers at a hospital in the Bronx — with professional musicians, and helping them write a lullaby for their own child. The parent brings the words. The musician helps with everything else. The song gets recorded, and the family keeps it.

              It has since spread to hospitals, shelters and correctional facilities in many more places. The premise is simple and it holds up: the most valuable lullaby a child can have is not the best-written one. It is the one their own parent made for them.

              Which is the entire idea behind the box above.

              And what we know now

              Three things worth knowing about babies and music.

              NeuroscienceWhat happens before they are born

              Hearing switches on somewhere around the third trimester — and by the time they arrive, they already know your voice.

              A much-cited 2013 study in PLOS ONE played a specific melody repeatedly to fetuses, then measured EEG responses after birth. The exposed babies responded differently to that melody than unexposed babies did — genuine prenatal learning, not just a startle reflex. A 2023 paper in Developmental Science went further, linking daily prenatal music to stronger neural encoding of the fundamental frequency of speech in newborns. In other words, music before birth may help lay the groundwork for processing speech sounds after it.

              Which means the songs you sing now are not going anywhere. They land somewhere, they stay, and one day they will be the thing that settles a crying newborn faster than anything else in the room.

              Good newsYou do not have to buy anything

              Somewhere along the way an entire industry grew up around the idea that the right CD played at the right bump produces a cleverer baby. You can let that one go. Nobody needs a boxed set.

              What genuinely shows up in the research is doing music rather than being played at it — years of singing, drumming on saucepans, and eventually learning an instrument if they want to. The active version is the one that matters, and it starts with the least expensive instrument in the house.

              Which is to say: your voice is the whole toolkit. Off-key is fine. Forgetting the second verse and inventing a worse one is fine. You are already the best-sounding thing they know.

              ClinicalMusic in the NICU

              This is the most evidence-dense corner of the whole subject, because the outcomes are measurable.

              A 2022 randomised trial in Pediatrics tested short-term music therapy for families with preterm infants. A Journal of Perinatology paper looked specifically at neonatal stress and pain. Systematic reviews have assessed whether live or recorded music measurably improves physiological stability — heart rate, oxygen saturation, feeding behaviour — in premature babies.

              The American Music Therapy Association publishes a plain-language fact sheet on NICU use with citations back to the primary studies, which is a good first stop if you ever need it.

              Archives and recordings

              Where the actual songs live, if you ever want to go listening.

              • Mama Lisa’s WorldThe loveliest thing on this list — children’s songs, lullabies and rhymes from all over, by country, usually with the original words and a translation
              • Carnegie Hall Lullaby ProjectThe real programme behind the writing box above: parents and musicians co-writing lullabies, with recordings and stories
              • Playing For ChangeSongs Around the World — one song, played by street musicians across dozens of countries and stitched together
              • The Global JukeboxAlan Lomax's cross-cultural database — about 5,700 songs coded across roughly 1,000 cultures, now public
              • Smithsonian FolkwaysEnormous world and traditional music archive, with education resources built for exactly this kind of digging
              • British Library SoundsAround 80,000 free recordings, including field recordings of lullabies and children's songs
              • Natural History of Song (Zenodo)The actual public audio corpus behind the Harvard study — 14-second excerpts coded by song type and culture
              • eHRAF World CulturesYale's structured ethnographic database; the lullaby-universality debate is built on this
              • UNESCO Intangible HeritageDocumented traditional music practices by culture, including many child-rearing traditions
